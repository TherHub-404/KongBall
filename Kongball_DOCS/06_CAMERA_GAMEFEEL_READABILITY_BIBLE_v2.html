Gameplay Engineering Bible · KB 06

Camera, Game Feel & Readability

The presentation layer that turns technically correct gameplay into an experience the player can immediately understand, control and enjoy.

Design target: The player should always know where they are, where the ball is, what just happened and what they can do next — while every important interaction feels impactful.

CORE PRINCIPLE
Camera, animation, VFX, audio and haptics are not decoration. They are part of the control system because they determine how the player perceives gameplay.

01 — The Perception Loop

PLAYER INPUT
 ↓
GAMEPLAY RESULT
 ↓
VISUAL / AUDIO / HAPTIC FEEDBACK
 ↓
PLAYER UNDERSTANDS RESULT
 ↓
NEXT INPUT

If the feedback is delayed, ambiguous or visually noisy, the game feels unresponsive even when the underlying code is correct.

02 — Camera Responsibilities

The camera must provide:

  • Player control orientation.
  • Ball awareness.
  • Spatial awareness.
  • Opponent awareness.
  • Goal awareness.
  • Readable framing.

03 — Camera Architecture

Camera Input
 ↓
Camera Intent
 ↓
Target Selection
 ↓
Desired Position / Rotation
 ↓
Collision Handling
 ↓
Smoothing
 ↓
Presentation

Camera should consume gameplay state. Gameplay systems should emit semantic events rather than directly owning camera transforms.

04 — Third-Person Follow

Expose:

  • Follow distance.
  • Height.
  • Pitch.
  • Yaw.
  • Follow sharpness.
  • Rotation sensitivity.
  • Look-ahead.

Do not add smoothing merely because smoothing looks "polished." Every delay has a gameplay cost.

05 — Camera-Relative Movement

Movement and camera must agree on what forward means.

Camera Forward + Input
        ↓
Flatten to ground plane
        ↓
Desired movement direction
        ↓
Character movement

If the camera rotates and the character suddenly feels like controls changed, the player loses trust.

06 — Ball Awareness

Football introduces a second critical object. The camera must keep enough context to locate the ball without constantly snapping toward it.

Possible signals:

  • Distance to ball.
  • Ball screen position.
  • Ball velocity.
  • Player velocity.
  • Goal direction.
  • Current action.

07 — Dynamic Framing

Use subtle dynamic framing:

NORMAL
Player centered

BALL FAR
Slightly widen framing

BALL AHEAD
Look-ahead

HIGH BALL
Increase vertical awareness

MAJOR IMPACT
Temporary presentation adjustment

Changes should be smooth and predictable.

08 — Camera Collision

The camera must not pass through the environment or create violent snaps.

Use collision avoidance with controlled minimum distance and smoothing. Test corners, walls, goals and crowded player clusters.

09 — Player Readability

Our cartoon monkeys must remain readable under chaotic conditions.

  • Strong silhouette.
  • Team color.
  • Readable body pose.
  • Visible ball.
  • Clear opponent/team distinction.
  • Readable action state.

10 — Visual Hierarchy

PriorityObject
1Player / immediate threat
2Ball
3Relevant opponents/teammates
4Goal / tactical destination
5Environment decoration

Decoration must never compete with gameplay-critical information.

11 — Game Feel

Game feel is the coordinated response to an action:

PLAYER ACTION
 ↓
GAMEPLAY EVENT
 ├── Animation
 ├── VFX
 ├── SFX
 ├── Camera
 └── Haptics

These systems should react to the same semantic event so that the experience feels like one coherent response.

12 — Hitstop

Very short impact pauses can emphasize major contacts. Use sparingly, especially in multiplayer.

Hitstop should enhance:

  • Power.
  • Contact clarity.
  • Comedy.

It must not make the controls feel sticky.

13 — Camera Shake

Camera shake is feedback, not noise.

Parameters:

  • Amplitude.
  • Duration.
  • Frequency.
  • Direction.
  • Falloff.

Small impacts should have small responses. Reserve strong shake for rare moments.

14 — Haptics

Controller vibration should correspond to semantic intensity:

SOFT CONTACT → subtle
KICK → directional pulse
STRONG TACKLE → strong short pulse
GOAL → celebratory pattern

Never use vibration continuously for ordinary movement.

15 — Audio as Spatial Feedback

Audio can communicate information the camera cannot:

  • Ball impact.
  • Opponent tackle.
  • Kick direction.
  • Goal.
  • Collision.
  • Danger.

Use spatialization where useful and avoid turning the soundscape into constant noise.

16 — VFX Rules

VFX should explain an event:

  • Ball trail communicates speed.
  • Impact burst communicates contact.
  • Dust communicates movement/landing.
  • Stun effect communicates state.
Never let VFX hide the ball or the player during a critical interaction.

17 — Cartoon Monkey Presentation

The monkey's exaggerated body can carry game feel:

  • Lean during acceleration.
  • Head lag.
  • Arm flail during impacts.
  • Tail follow-through.
  • Squash on landing.
  • Facial reaction on strong events.

The visual exaggeration should amplify the physical rules already defined by the gameplay system.

18 — Readability Under Chaos

Test with:

8 players12 players20 playersmultiple VFXball airborne

If the player cannot identify the ball or their own monkey, reduce visual complexity before adding more indicators.

19 — Camera Modes

  • Gameplay.
  • Respawn.
  • Goal.
  • Match intro.
  • Match result.
  • Celebration.

Mode changes must be explicit and driven by match state.

20 — Accessibility

  • Camera sensitivity.
  • Optional reduced camera shake.
  • Reduced motion.
  • Colorblind-friendly team distinction.
  • Vibration toggle.
  • UI scale.

22 — Automated Tests

  • Camera never clips through critical geometry.
  • Camera-relative movement remains consistent after camera rotation.
  • Ball remains readable during high-speed play.
  • Major impacts trigger coherent presentation events.
  • VFX cannot permanently obscure the ball.
  • Camera shake can be disabled independently.

23 — Anti-Patterns

  • Camera directly modifying gameplay movement.
  • Huge camera lag disguised as smoothing.
  • Camera constantly snapping between player and ball.
  • VFX used to compensate for unclear gameplay.
  • Excessive shake on ordinary collisions.
  • Audio/VFX systems each inventing their own impact timing.

24 — Senior Developer Decision Rules

01. The camera is part of gameplay.
02. Responsiveness beats cinematic smoothness during active play.
03. The ball must remain readable during chaos.
04. Feedback should be synchronized around semantic gameplay events.
05. Every visual effect has a readability cost.
06. Never fix bad gameplay with camera tricks.
07. Cartoon exaggeration should increase clarity, not visual noise.

25 — Definition of Done

  • Player can orient themselves instantly.
  • Ball remains readable.
  • Camera follows without fighting input.
  • Impacts feel powerful without excessive shake.
  • Animation, VFX, SFX and haptics respond coherently.
  • Monkey silhouettes remain readable with the target player count.
  • Accessibility controls exist for major motion effects.
FINAL PRINCIPLE
Perfect game feel is not a collection of effects. It is the absence of doubt between what the player did, what the game did, and what the player perceived.
