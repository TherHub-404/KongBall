Gameplay Engineering Bible · KB 01

Physics & Collision System

A senior-level specification for the physics, collision and impact layer of a third-person arcade football game with cartoon monkey characters.

Design target: Stumble Guys-like accessibility and physical comedy combined with football interactions that feel precise, readable and satisfying.

CORE PRINCIPLE
We are not simulating reality. We are simulating a gameplay reality that feels physically convincing, responds to player intent, remains fair, and produces spectacular moments.

01 — What Physics Is Responsible For

Physics is not "the system that makes everything move." It is the layer that answers questions such as:

  • Can these two objects occupy the same space?
  • What happens when they contact each other?
  • How does the ball respond to an impulse?
  • How does a player react to a tackle?
  • How does an object behave on a slope, wall or ramp?
  • How does the world constrain gameplay movement?

Physics should not decide game design by accident. If a physically correct result is bad gameplay, the gameplay system wins.

02 — The Four Layers

PLAYER INTENT
     ↓
GAMEPLAY RULES
     ↓
SIMULATION / PHYSICS
     ↓
PRESENTATION

Player Intent

What the player is trying to do: run, jump, tackle, kick, turn, chase the ball.

Gameplay Rules

What the game permits: tackle windows, knockback tiers, ball interaction radius, invulnerability, recovery, goal rules.

Simulation

Positions, velocities, contacts, impulses, collision resolution, gravity and other physical state.

Presentation

Animation, mesh deformation, VFX, audio, camera shake and haptics. Presentation communicates the simulation; it must not secretly redefine it.

Never reverse the dependency.
The monkey animation must not decide where the authoritative player is. The visible mesh follows gameplay state.

03 — Gameplay Physics vs Real Physics

Real-world mass, friction and momentum are useful mental models, but they are not our design constraints.

Realistic approachArcade approach
Accurate mass ratiosDesigner-controlled response strength
Physical frictionReadable braking and ball roll
Full rigidbody characterControlled character locomotion
Natural collision outcomeAuthored impact tiers
Emergent ragdoll everywhereRagdoll only when gameplay calls for it

The correct question is never "Is this physically accurate?" It is:

"Does this produce the intended player experience consistently?"

The character should normally be a controlled gameplay actor, not a loose rigidbody that is allowed to determine its own locomotion.

05 — The Character Collider

Use a simple, stable collider for the main body. The exact cartoon anatomy must not dictate gameplay collision.

The monkey may have an enormous head, long arms, huge feet and a tail.
None of those should accidentally make the player wider, taller or harder to control.

The collider should be:

  • Stable while running.
  • Stable during camera rotation.
  • Predictable near walls.
  • Simple enough for multiplayer simulation.
  • Independent from the render mesh.
  • Easy to visualize in debug mode.

06 — Collision Is Not Interaction

This distinction is foundational.

SystemPurpose
ColliderPhysical/environmental occupancy.
HurtboxReceives gameplay hits.
HitboxProduces a gameplay interaction during an active window.
Sensor / QueryDetects something without physically colliding.

A tackle should not require two full character colliders to physically slam together and hope the physics engine produces a good result.

Instead:

Tackle input
   ↓
Tackle state
   ↓
Tackle hitbox
   ↓
Find valid targets
   ↓
Gameplay interaction
   ↓
Apply authored response

07 — Collision Layers

Define a collision matrix before writing feature code.

PLAYER_BODY
PLAYER_HURTBOX
PLAYER_HITBOX
BALL
WORLD
GOAL
TRIGGER
SENSOR

Example design intent:

  • Player body collides with world.
  • Player body should not physically collide with its own hitboxes.
  • Hitboxes query hurtboxes.
  • Ball collides with world and appropriate gameplay geometry.
  • Triggers detect goals and boundaries.
  • Sensors detect nearby ball/player targets without creating physical forces.
Do not let collision configuration emerge organically as scripts are added. A chaotic collision matrix becomes one of the hardest bugs to diagnose later.

08 — Contact Resolution

When a collision occurs, do not immediately translate it into a force. First classify the contact.

CONTACT
  ↓
Who touched whom?
  ↓
Relative position / direction
  ↓
Relative velocity
  ↓
Current gameplay states
  ↓
Active action?
  ↓
Collision priority
  ↓
Choose response
  ↓
Apply response
  ↓
Emit presentation event

The response should be a deliberate gameplay decision.

09 — Relative Velocity

Impact strength should generally depend on how objects are moving relative to each other, not simply on the fact that two colliders touched.

A slow shoulder brush and a full-speed tackle should not have the same result.

Useful inputs to the impact model:

  • Relative velocity magnitude.
  • Direction of relative velocity.
  • Attacking player's current action.
  • Target's current state.
  • Mass or authored resistance.
  • Whether the target is grounded.
  • Cooldown / invulnerability state.

10 — Impact Tiers

Use a common language across the entire game:

TierResponseTypical use
0No gameplay reactionMinor contact
1Micro reactionSoft bump
2StaggerMedium collision
3StumbleStrong contact
4KnockbackHard tackle
5FallSevere impact
6RagdollBig, deliberate cartoon moment

These are gameplay states, not direct physics equations.

11 — Push vs Knockback

Do not treat every player collision as knockback.

Soft Push

Maintains player control and prevents overlapping.

Hard Push

Temporarily changes velocity but preserves control.

Knockback

Temporarily overrides normal locomotion.

Ragdoll

Suspends normal locomotion and transfers control to a controlled physical/animation reaction.

Each should have its own tuning parameters and recovery behaviour.

12 — Avoiding the "Physics Soup" Problem

A common failure mode in multiplayer arcade games is allowing every system to apply forces independently.

Movement → force
Tackle → force
Collision → force
Animation → movement
Ragdoll → force
Ball → force
Network correction → teleport
                ↓
          PHYSICS SOUP

Instead, use an explicit movement/impact authority.

Normal locomotion
      ↓
Action override
      ↓
Impact override
      ↓
Ragdoll
      ↓
Recovery
      ↓
Normal locomotion

Only the active authority should control the character's final gameplay velocity.

13 — Gravity

Gravity is a tuning parameter, not a sacred constant.

Expose at least:

  • Gravity strength.
  • Terminal fall speed.
  • Jump launch velocity.
  • Fall multiplier.
  • Air-control multiplier.

The desired perception is:

TAKEOFF → CLEAR APEX → DECISIVE FALL → CLEAN LANDING

Not necessarily a physically correct parabola.

14 — Grounding

Grounding is one of the highest-risk systems because tiny errors propagate into movement, animation, jumping and networking.

Ground detection should account for:

  • Ground normal.
  • Slope angle.
  • Small steps.
  • Edges.
  • Moving platforms.
  • Uneven terrain.
  • Temporary contact during impacts.
Never define grounded solely as velocity.y == 0.
Collision resolution can produce tiny numerical values that make this unreliable.

15 — Slopes

Movement should respect the surface normal where appropriate. Projecting intended movement onto the walkable ground plane prevents the character from visually floating or cutting into slopes.

Expose:

  • Maximum walkable slope.
  • Down-slope behaviour.
  • Up-slope acceleration modifier.
  • Sliding threshold, if used.

16 — Stairs & Steps

Cartoon characters must never visibly "fight" every tiny stair edge.

Define:

  • Maximum step height.
  • Step detection.
  • Step smoothing.
  • Landing behaviour.

Test with exaggerated monkey feet because the visual mesh and gameplay collider are intentionally different.

17 — Ball Physics Boundary

The ball deserves its own system. The physics layer provides the simulation, but football gameplay decides how the ball is contacted.

Player intent
   ↓
Ball interaction
   ↓
Kick / tackle / touch event
   ↓
Ball impulse or target velocity
   ↓
Physics simulation
   ↓
Collision response
   ↓
Ball gameplay state

Do not put ball logic inside the player movement controller.

18 — Ball Material Behaviour

The ball can use different tuned responses for:

  • Turf.
  • Wall.
  • Goal post.
  • Goal net.
  • Special arcade surfaces.

Do not chase realistic material values. Tune for:

  • Readability.
  • Predictability.
  • Match pacing.
  • Fun rebounds.

19 — Energy & Stability

Repeated collisions can accidentally inject energy into a simulation. This is especially dangerous with balls trapped between players, walls or corners.

Guard against:

  • Infinite bouncing.
  • Exploding velocities.
  • Player stacking.
  • Objects vibrating in corners.
  • Ball tunnelling at high speed.
  • Repeated collision callbacks applying the same effect.
Every physical interaction must have an upper bound.
If a bug can turn one kick into an infinite-energy ball, the system is not production-ready.

20 — Tunnelling & High-Speed Objects

Fast-moving balls and knockbacked players can cross a thin collider between simulation steps.

Explicitly test high-speed cases and use appropriate collision detection / sweep queries where required.

21 — Fixed Simulation & Frame Rate Independence

Gameplay physics must not become stronger or weaker because the render frame rate changed.

Test at:

30 FPS60 FPS120 FPSunstable FPS

Equivalent inputs should produce equivalent gameplay outcomes within the defined tolerance.

22 — Network Authority

For multiplayer, physics is not allowed to become a cheating surface.

Important gameplay events should be validated by the authoritative simulation:

  • Kick.
  • Tackle.
  • Knockback.
  • Goal.
  • Player position constraints.
  • Impossible acceleration/speed.

Client-side prediction may make local movement feel immediate, but the authoritative state remains the source of truth.

23 — Cartoon Physics

The monkeys are intentionally exaggerated. That is an advantage.

Use physics to create visual comedy:

  • Strong launch on heavy tackles.
  • Arms and legs flailing during impacts.
  • Large but controlled bounce reactions.
  • Brief squash on landing.
  • Ragdoll for spectacular failures.

However, cartoon physics must still obey the same gameplay rules every time.

Funny ≠ random.
The player should be surprised by what happened, not confused about why it happened.

24 — Physics ↔ Animation

Physics determines the gameplay event. Animation sells it.

TACKLE CONTACT
      ↓
Impact magnitude = 4
      ↓
Knockback state
      ↓
Animation: strong reaction
      ↓
VFX: impact burst
      ↓
SFX: heavy hit
      ↓
Camera: subtle shake
      ↓
Haptics

Do not solve weak animation by changing gameplay physics, and do not solve bad physics with animation that lies about the gameplay state.

25 — Physics ↔ Camera

Major physical events should emit semantic events rather than directly manipulating the camera from physics code.

Physics Event
   ↓
Impact Event
   ├── Animation
   ├── VFX
   ├── SFX
   ├── Camera
   └── Haptics

This keeps systems decoupled and lets the presentation layer change without rewriting simulation.

26 — Physics Debug Mode

The team must be able to see what the engine believes is happening.

Debug toggles should include:

  • Character collider.
  • Ball collider.
  • Hitboxes / hurtboxes.
  • Contact points.
  • Contact normals.
  • Velocity vectors.
  • Desired velocity.
  • Applied impulse.
  • Ground normal.
  • Current state.
  • Collision layer.
  • Ball trajectory.

28 — Automated Test Philosophy

Physics is difficult to test only by looking at the screen. Build tests around invariants.

  • Player never exceeds configured movement limits without an explicit state.
  • Diagonal input does not increase intended movement speed.
  • Ball velocity remains within configured safety bounds.
  • Repeated collision cannot generate unbounded energy.
  • Goal detection is deterministic.
  • Players can always recover from impact states.
  • Physics results are stable across target frame rates.
  • Changing a monkey skin does not change gameplay collider dimensions.
  • Network correction cannot create impossible gameplay states.

29 — Performance

Physics cost grows quickly with the number of players and interaction objects.

Prefer:

  • Simple colliders.
  • Explicit collision layers.
  • Queries instead of unnecessary physical bodies.
  • Object pooling for temporary effects.
  • Limited active ragdolls.
  • Controlled secondary physics.

Never optimize before profiling, but design the architecture so profiling has somewhere sensible to start.

30 — Anti-Patterns

  • Using the monkey mesh as the main collider.
  • Using a Rigidbody as the sole character movement system because it is "more realistic."
  • Letting every script directly modify transform position.
  • Letting every script directly add forces.
  • Putting ball logic inside PlayerController.
  • Using animation to teleport gameplay objects.
  • Relying on collision callbacks for all gameplay interactions.
  • Allowing physics to decide game rules.
  • Using random multipliers to hide systemic problems.
  • Adding ragdoll before normal impact states are stable.

31 — Senior Developer Decision Rules

01. Player agency beats physical realism.
02. Predictability beats emergent chaos in competitive interactions.
03. Surprise is good; unexplained behaviour is bad.
04. The mesh is never the authoritative gameplay body.
05. Use the simplest physical representation that creates the desired experience.
06. Every impact needs an authored response range.
07. No system may silently override another system's movement authority.
08. Physics-critical parameters belong in configuration, not magic numbers.
09. If a bug cannot be visualized, debugging the system is unnecessarily difficult.
10. Network-critical physics must be designed as network-critical from day one.
11. Never add complexity until the simpler model demonstrably fails.
12. A perfect physics system is one the player never has to think about.

32 — Definition of Done

The Physics & Collision system is not finished when the characters collide. It is finished when:

  • Movement remains stable under collision.
  • Ball physics are energetic but predictable.
  • Player impacts have a consistent language.
  • Cartoon reactions look spectacular without becoming random.
  • Players cannot become permanently stuck.
  • High-speed objects do not tunnel through the world.
  • Frame rate does not change gameplay behaviour.
  • Networking does not introduce impossible physical states.
  • All important interactions are debuggable.
  • The system can be tuned without rewriting code.
FINAL PRINCIPLE
The job of a senior gameplay engineer is not to build the most sophisticated physics system possible. It is to build the smallest, most controllable simulation that produces an extraordinary game.

This document is a design/engineering knowledge base for Claude. When implementing a feature, Claude should inspect the existing architecture first, identify the relevant physics authority, propose the smallest robust change, implement it, test edge cases, and only then tune values.
